Q3 Most likely: Christians.

Christianity sees grace as God's undeserved gift through Jesus (PBUH)'s sacrifice (Ephesians 2:8). Islam's equivalent is Rahma -- mercy and compassion -- from the same root as Rahim (womb). Islam teaches humanity was never broken. Adam (PBUH) and Eve sinned, God forgave them directly, and no debt carried forward. Every person is born pure (fitra).

Q4 Most likely: Christians. One of the most common verses used to argue for the divinity of Jesus (PBUH). Expect it regularly.

"What do you think about John 1:1, In the beginning was the Word?"

John 1:1 says: <i>In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.</i> The word translated as Word is the Greek Logos. Grammatically, the first two references to God use the definite article (ho theos), but the third does not. Many Greek scholars say this means <i>divine</i> or <i>godlike</i>, not identical to the Almighty God. John was written around 90-100 AD. The Trinity doctrine was formally defined at the Council of Nicaea in 325 AD, nearly 300 years after Jesus (PBUH).

The Islamic connection:

The Quran also calls Jesus (PBUH) the Word of God. Surah An-Nisa 4:171 calls him a Word from Him and Surah Al-Imran 3:45 describes him as a word from Allah. Islam understands this as God's creative command <i>Be!</i> (Kun). Jesus (PBUH) is an extraordinary creation of God, not God himself.

Three additional arguments to know:

1. It does not even prove a Trinity.

Even if you accept John 1:1 completely at face value, it only mentions God and the Word. The Holy Spirit appears nowhere in the verse. The most this verse could establish is a Duality, not a Trinity.

""Even if I take that verse exactly as it reads, it only mentions two: God and the Word. The Holy Spirit is nowhere in John 1:1. So the verse cannot establish a Trinity no matter how you read it. At most it would be a Duality. Why would the most important verse in defence of the Trinity not even mention all three?"

2. The 2 Corinthians 4:4 double standard.

The exact same Greek construction used in John 1:1 for the Word appears in 2 Corinthians 4:4 for the Devil: <i>the god of this world hath blinded the minds of them which believe not.</i> There, translators wrote a lowercase god. In John 1:1 the same Greek is rendered as God with a capital G. That is a translation choice driven by doctrine, not by grammar.

""The exact same Greek word used in John 1:1 for the Word appears in 2 Corinthians 4:4 for the Devil. But there it gets translated as lowercase god. Same word, different treatment. That tells you the translation was shaped by the conclusion the translator wanted to reach, not by the original Greek."

3. The Bible calls other humans gods too.

Psalms 82:6: <i>I have said, ye are gods; and all of you are children of the most High.</i> Exodus 7:1: God tells Moses <i>I have made you a god to Pharaoh.</i> Jesus (PBUH) himself quoted Psalms 82:6 in John 10:34 to make this exact point.

""The Bible uses the word god for humans elsewhere. God tells Moses in Exodus 7:1: I have made you a god to Pharaoh. Psalms 82:6 says ye are gods to ordinary people. Jesus (PBUH) himself quoted Psalms 82:6 in John 10:34. So when John 1:1 says the Word was divine, it fits perfectly within that tradition without requiring the Word to be God Almighty himself."

Opening response:

""The word translated as Word is Logos in Greek, which had a very specific philosophical meaning for the Greek-speaking audience John was writing for. Interestingly, the Quran also calls Jesus (PBUH) the Word of God. We do not dispute that title at all. A word comes from a speaker. It expresses the speaker, but it is not the speaker himself."

If they push further:

""Look at the same Gospel of John. In John 14:28 Jesus (PBUH) says: The Father is greater than I. In John 5:30: I can of my own self do nothing. And in John 20:17: my God and your God. He calls God his own God. How do we reconcile those with him being God himself?"

Q5 Most likely: Christians. Comes up regularly alongside John 1:1.

"What do you think about Alpha and Omega? Does that prove Jesus is God?"

Alpha and Omega are the first and last letters of the Greek alphabet, meaning the Beginning and the End. It appears exclusively in the Book of Revelation (1:8, 1:17-18, 21:6, 22:13). Revelation 1:8 explicitly identifies the speaker as Lord God and the Almighty -- titles for God the Father, not Jesus (PBUH). The title also already exists in the Old Testament: Isaiah 44:6 says <i>I am the first and the last; apart from me there is no God.</i>

The Islamic connection:

Surah Al-Hadid 57:3: <i>He is the First and the Last, the Ascendant and the Intimate.</i> Al-Awwal (The First) and Al-Akhir (The Last) are two of the 99 names of Allah. Islam agrees completely that God is the Beginning and the End.

Opening response:

""We actually love that title. The Quran says exactly the same thing in Surah Al-Hadid: He is the First and the Last. Al-Awwal and Al-Akhir are two of the names of God in Islam. We agree completely that God is the Alpha and the Omega. What I would point out is that in Revelation 1:8, the clearest use of that title, the verse itself says it belongs to Lord God, the Almighty. And the same title appears in Isaiah 44:6 centuries before Jesus (PBUH), where God says: I am the first and the last, apart from me there is no God."

If they point to Revelation 22:13 specifically:

""Read three verses further. In Revelation 22:16, Jesus (PBUH) speaks again and introduces himself separately: I, Jesus, have sent my angel to give you this testimony. If he was already speaking in verse 13 as the Alpha and Omega, why does he introduce himself again as a different speaker in verse 16? Many scholars read verse 13 as God the Father and verse 16 as Jesus (PBUH) speaking as His messenger."

Q6 Most likely: Christians. John 14:6 comes up frequently in dawah.

"Jesus said I am the way, the truth and the life. Does that not prove he is God?"

The full verse is: <i>Jesus answered, I am the way and the truth and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me.</i> (John 14:6). This was said privately to his disciples at the Last Supper. The verse itself makes the distinction clear: he is the way <i>to the Father</i>. The Father is still above him and separate. A way to God is not the same as being God.

John is full of these I am statements. Jesus (PBUH) also says <i>I am the vine</i> (John 15:1), <i>I am the bread of life</i> (John 6:35), and <i>I am the door</i> (John 10:9). Nobody takes those as literal claims. They are metaphorical statements of purpose and role, not declarations of divinity.

The same writer behind the Gospel of John also wrote Acts. In Acts 10:34-35, Peter says: <i>In every nation, whoever fears God and works righteousness is accepted by Him.</i> That opens salvation far wider than a single verse in John suggests.

Opening response:

""That verse is actually saying he is the way to the Father. Look at the verse itself: No one comes to the Father except through me. The Father is still above and distinct. Jesus (PBUH) is pointing beyond himself toward God. That is exactly how the Quran describes him too, as one of the greatest Messengers Allah ever sent, a path to God."

If they say it proves Jesus is the only way:

""John 14:6 was said privately to his disciples at the Last Supper, not as a universal decree. And Acts 10:34-35 in the same body of writing says: in every nation, whoever fears God and works righteousness is accepted by Him. That sounds much broader. How do you reconcile those two from the same author?"

If they say the I am statements prove divinity:

""In the same Gospel, Jesus (PBUH) also says I am the vine and I am the bread of life. Those are clearly metaphorical. The I am statements describe his role and purpose, not his nature. He is the way to God, like a door is the way into a room. The door is not the same as what is inside."

The Islamic connection:

Islam agrees that Jesus (PBUH) is a way to God. He is one of the five greatest Messengers in Islam (Ulul Azm), alongside Noah, Abraham, Moses, and Muhammad (peace be upon them all). Quran 4:80: <i>Whoever obeys the Messenger has obeyed Allah.</i>

Q7 Most likely: Christians. The Trinity is the central belief separating Christianity from Islam. Expect it in almost every conversation.

"The Father, Son and Holy Spirit are three but one. How do you respond to the Trinity?"

The word Trinity does not appear anywhere in the Bible. Not once. It was formally defined at the Council of Nicaea in 325 AD, three centuries after Jesus (PBUH). Before Nicaea, many early Christians (the Arians) believed Jesus (PBUH) was a created being, subordinate to God. The doctrine was settled by a vote at a council called by the Roman Emperor Constantine, not by the disciples or Jesus (PBUH) himself.

The closest verse used to support it is 1 John 5:7 (KJV): <i>For there are three that bear record in heaven, the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost, and these three are one.</i> Most modern biblical scholars acknowledge this verse was not in the original Greek manuscripts. It was added by later scribes.

Three powerful questions to ask:

First:

Did Jesus (PBUH) ever say "I am God, worship me" anywhere in the Bible? He never did. He said worship the Lord your God (Matthew 4:10). He prayed to God. He called God his God (John 20:17). A being who is God does not pray to God.

Second:

If the Father and the Son are equally and fully God, why does Jesus (PBUH) say in Mark 13:32 that not even the Son knows the hour of judgment, only the Father? How can God not know something that God knows? That is not one being. That is a hierarchy.

Third:

In the Garden of Gethsemane, Jesus (PBUH) prayed and said <i>not my will, but yours be done</i> (Luke 22:42). If the Father and Son are one God with one will, whose will was different from whose? Two wills means two beings.

Opening response:

""We believe in the same God you do. We just believe God is one, without partners or divisions, exactly as Jesus (PBUH) himself taught. In Mark 12:29 a scholar asked Jesus what the greatest commandment is, and he answered: Hear O Israel, the Lord our God, the Lord is One. That is pure monotheism. That is exactly what Muslims say in the Shahada. Jesus (PBUH) and Muslims are saying the same thing."

If they say the Trinity is a mystery beyond human understanding:

""I respect that. But a belief that cannot be explained or understood by any human, and that Jesus (PBUH) himself never taught in those words, is worth examining carefully. God sent Prophets so we could understand Him, not so we could believe in things that contradict reason."

If they quote 1 John 5:7:

""Most modern Bible translations remove that verse or add a footnote saying it does not appear in the earliest Greek manuscripts. Biblical scholars, including Christian ones, broadly agree it was added by later scribes."

The Islamic position:

Surah Al-Maidah 5:73: <i>Surely they have disbelieved who say God is the third of three. There is no god but One God.</i> Surah Al-Ikhlas (112): <i>Say He is Allah the One. Allah the Eternal Refuge. He neither begets nor was begotten. And there is nothing comparable to Him.</i>

Key References

  • John 20:17 -- Jesus (PBUH): my God and your God

"Was Jesus (PBUH) crucified and did he die for everyone's sins?"

Deuteronomy 24:16: <i>Parents are not to be put to death for their children, nor children for their parents. Matthew 12:39-40: <i>No sign will be given except the sign of the prophet Jonah. He came out alive. The sign of Jonah is survival, not death.

Argument 7: Isaiah 53 is about Israel, not Jesus (PBUH).

The servant in Isaiah 53 is identified as Israel in the previous chapters. Isaiah 41:8: <i>You, Israel, my servant.</i> Isaiah 43:10: <i>You are my witnesses and my servant whom I have chosen.</i> Isaiah 49:3: <i>You are my servant, Israel.</i> The same servant continues into chapter 53. The suffering described is Israel in Babylonian exile. There is not a single pre-Christian Jewish interpretation of Isaiah 53 as a suffering Messiah.

Argument 8: The Gospels contradict each other on the crucifixion.

What were Jesus (PBUH)'s last words? Matthew and Mark say: <i>My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?</i> Luke says: <i>Father, into your hands I commit my spirit.</i> John says: <i>It is finished.</i> Who carried the cross? Matthew, Mark and Luke say Simon of Cyrene. John says Jesus (PBUH) carried it himself. What time was he crucified? Mark says the third hour (9am). John says the sixth hour (noon). And the Q source -- which Matthew and Luke both drew from -- contains no crucifixion account at all.

What I find fascinating is that even in the Bible, Deuteronomy 24:16 says no one can die for another person's sin. Ezekiel 18:20 repeats it. Hosea 6:6 says God desires mercy, not sacrifice. And Jesus (PBUH) himself said the only sign he would give is the sign of Jonah, who came out of the whale alive. The four Gospels also give three different versions of his last words. These are honest questions worth sitting with. Islam says Allah saved Jesus (PBUH), raised him, and honoured him. That is actually a more honourable outcome for such a great Prophet."

Four Gospels. Four different accounts of the single most important moment in the faith. If these were eyewitness records, they would not differ this significantly on who was present, what was seen, and who Jesus (PBUH) appeared to first.
